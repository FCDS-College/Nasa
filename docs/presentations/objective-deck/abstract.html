Flame in Freefall: Turning NASA Microgravity Combustion Data into Sourced Fire-Safety Answers for Spacecraft Cabins

Team Flame in Freefall
FCDS College · November 2026
Abstract. Fire behaves differently in a spacecraft. Without buoyancy, flames are driven only by ventilation, can persist at lower oxygen and spread in ways that ground tests do not predict, while the next exploration vehicles plan richer atmospheres: up to 30 % (Orion) and 34 % (Altair) oxygen at reduced pressure, against 21 % on the ISS. NASA has flown decades of microgravity combustion experiments, but their results are scattered across experiment tables, metadata records and technical reports, so a mission designer cannot get a direct, sourced answer to the question “what do we know about fire in my cabin atmosphere?” We present Flame in Freefall, a fire-safety dashboard that, for any chosen cabin atmosphere, ranks NASA flight investigations by how directly their data covers it, summarizes each in NASA’s own words, explains what the flight tests measured with every number linked to its source table cell, estimates droplet-flame outcomes with a validated model that refuses to extrapolate, and states what the data cannot tell. Built on 840 flight tests from four NASA Physical Sciences Informatics investigations (FLEX, BASS-II, SPICE, SAFFIRE-I) and 24 public-use NTRS reports, the system reaches a macro-F1 of 0.54 (baseline 0.27) and an extinction ROC-AUC of 0.86 for flame outcomes, and a retrieval hit@1 of 0.88 on a 26-question gold set. Treating the absence of data as a reported gap, never as absence of risk, it gives designers sourced evidence and visible testing gaps before an atmosphere is chosen.
Keywords: microgravity combustion; spacecraft fire safety; cabin atmosphere; evidence ranking; retrieval-augmented generation; applicability domain; NASA PSI.

1Problem

Spacecraft fire safety depends on how materials and fuels burn in the cabin’s own atmosphere and gravity. In microgravity there is no rising plume: flames are pushed only by ventilation, can survive at lower oxygen, and spread differently from ground tests [1]. Exploration vehicles shift the atmosphere as well; NASA/TM-2008-215260 records up to 30 % and 34 % O2 at reduced pressure for Orion and Altair designs, compared with 21 % at 14.7 psia on the ISS [1]. Higher oxygen makes materials burn more readily.

The relevant evidence exists, from flight programmes such as BASS, FLEX, Saffire, SPICE and ACME, but it is fragmented: one table row per test, ISA-Tab metadata per investigation, and hundreds of reports, some with unreadable text layers. Nothing connects this evidence to an atmosphere a designer is considering, and nothing shows where the evidence stops.

2Objective

To summarize, rank and explain NASA microgravity combustion results so that they inform fire safety for Moon and Mars missions. The question is framed around the atmosphere rather than the experiment: given O2 fraction and total pressure (four NASA-sourced presets or a custom value), which flight data covers it, what did it show, and what remains untested?

3Approach

  • Data. 840 CC0 flight tests (FLEX 274, BASS-II 129, SPICE 435, SAFFIRE-I 2), their ISA-Tab metadata, 24 NTRS reports and flight media [2, 3]. Every value keeps the identifier of its source cell; OCR recovers 35 garbled report pages.
  • Ranking. A transparent evidence-relevance score with four visible components: O2 coverage, pressure coverage, nearby tests and outcome data. It is presented as relevance, never as risk.
  • Outcome model. Multinomial logistic regression on O2, pressure, ppO2, CO2, He, droplet size and fuel, trained on 261 FLEX tests over 80 test days and selected over random forest and kNN by repeated grouped cross-validation. A strict applicability domain makes it refuse outside flown conditions.
  • Retrieval and chat. PostgreSQL full-text and pgvector search fused by reciprocal rank fusion, then reranked by a cross-encoder; answers may cite only retrieved evidence.
  • Knowledge graph. 951 entities and 10,683 edges extracted deterministically from the tables, each citing its NASA cell.

For a lunar-lander atmosphere (34 % O2, 62.7 kPa), FLEX ranks first with nine tests flown nearby; BASS-II’s solid-material tests stop at 22.2 % O2 and are reported as a coverage gap; and the outcome model declines to estimate, because 62.7 kPa lies below the lowest FLEX test pressure (70.2 kPa). Oxygen is the dominant model driver, followed by He and CO2 diluents, with physically expected signs.

5Limitations

Outcomes are modelled for droplets only; no partial-gravity (Moon, Mars) data is included; SAME, ACME and FLEX-2 are not yet ingested; generated chat answers were not evaluated; and the ranking weights and gold set await expert review.

6Conclusion

Flame in Freefall converts scattered NASA flight evidence into ranked, cited answers for a specific cabin atmosphere, and reports the limits of that evidence explicitly. Future work will extract BASS-II spread and extinction from flight video, add Saffire II–VI and ACME, and calibrate the relevance weights against expert judgement.
